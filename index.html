Mechanism

The lag is not hesitation and it is not risk aversion — both of those would vary with stakes, and yours doesn't. What you are doing in those 1.8 days is constructing the version of the decision that will survive being questioned. That is why it's invariant: it isn't a response to the situation, it's a response to being accountable for it.

Which means the standard advice — "be more decisive" — will fail, and has, twice. The lag isn't a gap in courage you can close with a rep.

A correction this page owes you

An earlier version of this readout called the lag an invariant and told you to stop working on it. That overreached, and by the rule stated below it was wrong. The test for an invariant is a rep that targeted it and failed. Your two commitments aimed at adjacent behaviors — naming the information holder, and saying the unsoftened thing. Neither aimed at the lag. It has never actually been tried.

The mechanism above is still the best available explanation, and it may well prove unchangeable. But telling you to stop working on something we never properly tested would risk capping you on the strength of an assumption. So: candidate invariant, one rep from a verdict.

What to do in the meantime

Two structural moves that pay off either way: pre-authorise the decision one level down so the 1.8 days happens in parallel rather than in series, and name in advance the two or three calls where you will spend the lag deliberately. Your 1.8 days is only expensive when it's unplanned. If the targeted rep then moves it, you have both. If it doesn't, you already have the scaffolding.

Gap or invariant — the test

Both look identical from outside: a number that stays low across runs. The ledger separates them. If a rep specifically targeted it and it still didn't move, it's an invariant — design around it. If no rep ever aimed at it properly, it's still a gap — keep working it.

Information-seeking is a gap: the one rep aimed at it hit the noticing half, so the behavior itself has never been targeted. The lag is a candidate: stable across everything measured, and never targeted either. Neither has earned the label "unchangeable." Rep option B on this page is the one that would settle it.

You will be well-informed about anything that arrives on a dashboard, in a report, or from someone who likes their job. What four runs show

Across 812 events you delivered 61 pieces of genuinely bad news. 48 were direct to the point of bluntness — including to a board chair, a regulator and a majority investor, none of whom you had any incentive to be blunt with. The 13 you softened share one property and only one: in every case, the recipient had already absorbed a cost from a decision you made. See all 61, sorted

Grady, whose season you ended. Reyes, whose rotation you decided without him. Delacroix, whose plant you closed in Overdrive. You are not avoiding conflict. Mechanism

Softening someone you've already hurt feels like decency and functions as avoidance. The message gets vaguer exactly when the person most needs it clear, and they read the vagueness accurately — as guilt, or as being managed. In all three cases the recipient learned the real content from a third party within 48 hours. Every one of them told the exit log they'd have preferred it straight from you.

Why this correction is worth more than the original finding

"Be more direct" is unusable advice for you — you already are, 79% of the time. The usable version is narrow and hard: the people you've cost something are the people who most need it straight from you, and they are the exact people you'll want to cushion. That's one specific, recognizable moment rather than a general personality note.

What the ledger establishes — and what it doesn't

Three commitments is n=3, thin enough that the direction is worth naming and the conclusion isn't. What can be said: the one commitment you kept produced your largest single-dimension gain, and the one with no daily rep preceded a decline.

What cannot yet be said is that this is how you work. Each of those is a single instance. “Awareness alone doesn't move you” is one observation, not a law — it agrees with a great deal of behavior-change research, which is why it's worth acting on, but this ledger is not the evidence for it. Two more commitments and it becomes yours rather than borrowed.

The instructive case is the partial one. You did the rep faithfully — 24 of 30 — and the number moved 5 points, because the rep trained the half you were already good at. A rep aimed at the wrong half of a behavior produces consistency without change. That mechanism is worth more to you than the ledger's arithmetic.
