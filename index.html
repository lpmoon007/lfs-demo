Mechanism

The lag is not hesitation and it is not risk aversion — both of those would vary with stakes, and yours doesn't. What you are doing in those 1.8 days is constructing the version of the decision that will survive being questioned. That is why it's invariant: it isn't a response to the situation, it's a response to being accountable for it.

Which means the standard advice — "be more decisive" — will fail, and has, twice. The lag isn't a gap in courage you can close with a rep.

A correction this page owes you

An earlier version of this readout called the lag an invariant and told you to stop working on it. That overreached, and by the rule stated below it was wrong. The test for an invariant is a rep that targeted it and failed. Your two commitments aimed at adjacent behaviors — naming the information holder, and saying the unsoftened thing. Neither aimed at the lag. It has never actually been tried.

The mechanism above is still the best available explanation, and it may well prove unchangeable. But telling you to stop working on something no rep has ever targeted would risk capping you on the strength of an assumption. So: candidate invariant, one rep from a verdict.

What to do in the meantime

Two structural moves that pay off either way: pre-authorise the decision one level down so the 1.8 days happens in parallel rather than in series, and name in advance the two or three calls where you will spend the lag deliberately. Your 1.8 days is only expensive when it's unplanned. If the targeted rep then moves it, you have both. If it doesn't, you already have the scaffolding.

Gap or invariant — the test

Both look identical from outside: a number that stays low across runs. The ledger separates them. If a rep specifically targeted it and it still didn't move, it's an invariant — design around it. If no rep ever aimed straight at it, it's still a gap — keep working it.

Information-seeking is a gap: the one rep aimed at it hit the noticing half, so the behavior itself has never been targeted. The lag is a candidate: stable across everything measured, and never targeted either. Neither has earned the label "unchangeable." Rep option B on this page is the one that would settle it.

How a page like this gets built

Twelve scenarios. Fourteen months. No questionnaire at any point.

Each run is a live crisis you lead in your own words — a mountain, an airline, a grid failure, a factory. Your team brings you what it holds, or holds it back, depending on how you have treated people. Everything you do is logged: who you contacted, what you didn't open, how long you took, what you softened.

One run gives you a sharp, same-day debrief — no questionnaire, no personality type, just what you actually did. Keep going and it deepens: the measurement that holds across every scenario, the condition that splits you in two, the claims your own record overturns, and a ledger of whether you change when you say you will. The profile above had run four — but the trust doesn't come from the number, it comes from every line tracing to a decision you made.

And there's no finish line. The more scenarios you run, the more accurate the read — and you can re-run the same one on a harder setting to test yourself against it again. A memory layer carries what you did last time forward, so each run presses harder where you are weakest instead of repeating itself.
